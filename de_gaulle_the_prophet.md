# De Gaulle's Military Prophecy: The Warning That France Ignored

## A Prophet in Context

In 1934, amid the economic turmoil of the Depression, a slim volume appeared in Paris bookshops: *Vers l'Armée de Métier* (Toward a Professional Army), written by Lieutenant-Colonel Charles de Gaulle. This modest text would prove to be among the most remarkable military prophecies of the twentieth century, though few recognized it at the time.

De Gaulle's work emerged from a broader international debate on mechanization that had been building since the Great War. In Britain, theorists like J.F.C. Fuller and B.H. Liddell Hart had advocated for mobile armored warfare in numerous articles. In France itself, General Jean Estienne, "father of the tank," had outlined potential tank tactics in his 1921 memorandum. Soviet military thinkers like Triandafillov were simultaneously developing "deep battle" concepts. De Gaulle synthesized these currents while adding crucial original elements: the call for an elite volunteer corps and a political argument for restructuring France's army.

Meanwhile, French military doctrine in the 1930s remained anchored to the memory of Verdun, where hundreds of thousands had perished in the trenches of the Great War. The General Staff adhered to the concept of *la bataille conduite* (methodical battle), a deliberate step-by-step approach emphasizing firepower over maneuver. Politicians allocated funds to the Maginot Line and counted on millions of reservists to dig in behind it when war returned. The army's "long-war" mindset assumed time for mobilization that modern warfare would not permit. Tanks remained mere auxiliaries, scattered in small parcels and limited to infantry pace.

## A Clear Alternative

De Gaulle's argument was direct: future wars would be won by nations able to field a completely motorized striking force that could break an opponent's center before mobilization schedules caught up. He proposed six fully armored divisions totaling approximately one hundred thousand long-service volunteers. Each division would have its own engineers, artillery, signals teams, and aviation support. This force would not be scattered across the front like France's existing tank battalions but concentrated for breakthrough operations.

Once such a corps pierced the enemy line, it would maintain momentum, advancing continuously under the guidance of wireless communications and protected by air cover. The book included detailed tables of costs, personnel requirements, and organizational structures. De Gaulle estimated the annual cost at approximately three billion francs—roughly equivalent to what France was spending yearly on the Maginot Line—and specified the tempo of operations that would be required for decisive effect, a pace that directly challenged the methodical battle doctrine.

Where French military thinking emphasized firepower delivered in sequential phases, de Gaulle stressed tempo, speed, and continuous momentum—concepts that fundamentally clashed with the General Staff's approach to warfare. His emphasis on professional soldiers rather than conscripts also ran counter to France's deeply-held commitment to the citizen-soldier model.

## Reception in France

The reception in France was largely dismissive. Contemporary newspapers treated the project as propaganda, the speculative work of an ambitious officer. Marshal Philippe Pétain, the venerated hero of Verdun and de Gaulle's former mentor, testified against the plan, characterizing it as impractical and unnecessary. Though Paul Reynaud's bill based on de Gaulle's proposals passed the Chamber of Deputies on November 13, 1934 (by a vote of 289-249), it was shelved by the Senate on March 20, 1935.

The political establishment focused on the cost rather than the strategic advantage. Military leadership, dominated by generals whose experiences came from the trenches of the Great War, remained committed to defensive doctrines that had eventually secured victory in 1918, albeit at terrible cost. De Gaulle's impatient, sometimes abrasive manner earned him few allies within the general staff, further isolating his ideas from serious consideration.

Political fears also played a crucial role. Both the Left and Right harbored deep suspicions of a professional "praetorian" army that might threaten parliamentary control. The memory of General Boulanger's 1889 political crisis—when a popular general nearly seized power—remained a potent warning against creating an elite military force disconnected from the citizen-conscript tradition.

While France dismissed its prophet, across the Rhine his work found more attentive readers.

## German Interest

A German translation of *Vers l'Armée de Métier* was published by Mittler-Verlag in late 1935 and circulated in the Wehrmacht's War Academy in Berlin. Officers experimenting with fast armor tactics noted that a Frenchman had articulated concepts remarkably similar to their own emerging doctrine. Heinz Guderian, who would later lead the panzer forces through the Ardennes, included de Gaulle's work in his War Academy lecture cycle of 1936, describing it as "interessant aber politisch unmöglich" (interesting but politically impossible)—recognizing both its military insight and the institutional barriers preventing its implementation in France.

The Wehrmacht incorporated many of these mechanized warfare concepts into their operational planning. What French authorities dismissed as impractical, German officers viewed as the future of warfare. They studied these ideas not as theoretical exercises but as practical doctrine, adapting them to their own strategic needs.

Meanwhile, France did not entirely ignore mechanized warfare. Beginning in 1935, the army created its first Light Mechanized Division (1ère DLM), and in January 1940, just months before the German attack, it formed several Armored Divisions (DCRs). However, these units were too few, too new, and critically, employed according to the methodical battle doctrine rather than concentrated for decisive breakthrough operations. By 1940, France possessed approximately 3,000 modern tanks—numerically superior to Germany's 2,800—but only about 10 percent were equipped with radios, compared to nearly all German tanks. This communications gap would prove decisive in the coming battle.

## Spanish Civil War: The Dress Rehearsal

The Spanish Civil War (1936-1939) provided a critical testing ground for armored warfare concepts. The German Condor Legion deployed tank units that validated the radio-directed, concentrated armor approach that de Gaulle had advocated. Operating with close air support and maintaining continuous communications, these units demonstrated the potential of mechanized warfare even on difficult terrain.

French military observers drew different conclusions from the same conflict, focusing on the need for heavier armor to withstand enemy fire rather than the decisive potential of speed, concentration, and coordinated operations. This misreading of the Spanish "dress rehearsal" further reinforced France's commitment to methodical battle doctrine when it should have served as a warning.

## May 1940

When the test came in May 1940, events unfolded with striking similarity to de Gaulle's analysis. Armored corps under Generals Ewald von Kleist and Heinz Guderian thrust through the Ardennes Forest, crossed the Meuse River at Sedan on May 13-14, and achieved what the French high command had deemed impossible: they outpaced every French reserve unit sent to block them and reached the English Channel near Abbeville by May 20.

Britain's small professional army escaped at Dunkirk during the evacuation that ran from May 26 to June 4, but the French state—considered by many military experts to possess the strongest army in Europe—collapsed after only six weeks of fighting. On June 22, 1940, France signed an armistice, a collapse that matched the rapid timeframe de Gaulle had warned about. The defensive strategy he had criticized proved exactly as vulnerable as he had predicted.

In a tragic irony, de Gaulle himself received command of France's 4th Armored Division (4e DCR) shortly before the German attack. Between May 17 and May 29, he led counterattacks at Montcornet and Abbeville that demonstrated the potential of concentrated armor operations. These limited successes provided a real-world "proof of concept" for his theories, but the division was too small and deployed too late to alter the course of the campaign.

## Prophetic Elements

Several specific elements that de Gaulle emphasized in 1934 proved remarkably accurate. He wrote that wireless sets mounted in every tank would be decisive because artillery and aircraft could be directed in real time. In 1940, Stuka dive-bomber groups and Henschel 126 liaison aircraft answered calls from panzer units with devastating efficiency, while French armor remained largely without effective communications. Most French tanks had no radios at all; only the command variant of the SAu-40 carried a high-power set, while some others had only receiver sets, rendering them unable to coordinate with supporting arms.

De Gaulle warned that conscript commanders, hastily recalled from civilian life, could never absorb the tempo of mechanized warfare. During the 1940 campaign, many French officers met their own troops for the first time on mobilization day, creating predictable problems for unit cohesion and command effectiveness.

He also argued that prioritizing static fortifications over mobile forces would leave the nation unable to regain initiative once the frontier was breached. By June 1940, this prediction had proven accurate. Paris had no force capable of mounting an effective counterattack. The French Army, scattered and disorganized, could not concentrate sufficient combat power to strike back decisively against the German advance.

De Gaulle had also addressed aviation in his book (on pages 112-114), outlining the need for close air support for armored operations. The Luftwaffe delivered exactly this capability through dedicated liaison units, though at a scale far greater than de Gaulle had envisioned, showing that he had identified the right concept but underestimated the resources required.

## Limitations and Accuracy

Not every element of de Gaulle's forecast proved perfect. He envisioned heavy tanks like the French Char B1 (which weighed 32 tons, not the 45 tons sometimes claimed; the improved B1 bis version reached 36 tons) as the main instruments of breakthrough, whereas the Germans relied on lighter, more maneuverable models that sacrificed armor for speed. The Panzer III and IV tanks that formed the backbone of the German armored divisions were lighter than de Gaulle had anticipated, though their tactical employment followed his conceptual framework.

De Gaulle also understated the scale of air power required to sustain deep armored penetrations. The Luftwaffe's role in the 1940 campaign proved more decisive than he had predicted, with German air superiority paralyzing French response efforts and severing communication lines. Even so, his central diagnosis—that a professional mechanized spearhead could topple a continental power before its mass conscript army could effectively deploy—proved accurate.

## Why France Ignored Him

Why did France ignore these clear warnings? The trauma of the previous war had created a psychological attachment to static defense. The idea of never again suffering the massive casualties of offensive operations seemed worth almost any price. The professional corps that de Gaulle proposed carried a cost that the Treasury, still reeling from the Depression, would not meet. De Gaulle's own impatience earned him few allies inside the general staff, whose support he needed to advance his ideas.

The methodical battle doctrine that dominated French military thinking represented more than just tactical preference; it reflected a deeply-held institutional culture that valued deliberate action, firepower, and caution over the tempo and risk that de Gaulle advocated. This doctrinal commitment proved remarkably resistant to change, even in the face of mounting evidence from Spain and elsewhere.

The irony is that one of the clearest warnings in European military history came from a serving officer whose address, telephone number, and evidence were all in the public domain. Another country read the book, acted on it, and reaped the results. France, meanwhile, dismissed the prophet in its midst until it was too late.

## Historical Assessment

Within the field of operational military doctrine, de Gaulle's foresight stands at a remarkable height. He described how an enemy would destroy his own nation, and he did so with precision that allows his description to be laid beside the fall of France point by point.

In any assessment of the twentieth century's most impressive predictions, de Gaulle's 1934 manifesto belongs near the top, both for what it saw coming and for the tragic clarity with which it was ignored. The serving officer whose warnings were studied in Berlin while dismissed in Paris would, by history's strange turns, become the leader who would rebuild the nation whose fall he had foretold.
